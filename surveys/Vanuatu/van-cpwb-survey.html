Culturally Protected Area Survey in Vanuatu

Help us document tabu areas — Vanuatu culturally protected areas

In Vanuatu, customary tabu areas have long been placed on reefs, freshwaters, and lands to manage resources of cultural, social, and subsistence value. Two well-known forms of this practice are funerary tabu areas — placed on a clan’s reef or land following the death of a chief, or of any clan member (man, woman, or child) — and circumcision tabu areas, placed as part of cleansing rites for boys’ circumcision rituals. Funerary closures may last from a year to many years depending on the rank of the deceased, while circumcision closures are typically shorter, sometimes a month, but particularly effective when they coincide with spawning aggregations (Hickey, 2006).

While these two tabu area practices are known from Hickey’s work in Vanuatu, much less is known about where they are practised and the extent to which they are still observed across the archipelago — the kind of detailed, community-level picture that has now been documented for Fiji’s five culturally protected water body practices (Vave, 2022). This survey aims to fill that gap for Vanuatu.

Benefits: Documenting and promoting these traditional management systems helps advocate for greater recognition, strengthening and support of indigenous management in Vanuatu and the region. Tabu areas can produce larger and more abundant marine organisms, export larvae and propagules, generate spillover effects, and protect a diversity of habitats and species — while remaining endogenously sanctioned by community belief systems rather than relying on outside enforcement (Hickey, 2006).

Risks: As Hickey notes, the trend in Vanuatu has been a gradual shift from a primarily culturally motivated regime of resource management to a more commercially motivated system, with the implementation and sanctioning of taboos becoming increasingly less reliant on traditional beliefs and practices. Many tabu places are no longer respected by younger generations due to the influence of Christianity, Western education, and development pressure, and the abandonment of these practices is sometimes cited as a reason for resource depletion (Hickey, 2006).

Consent to Participate in Research

University of Hawaiʻi Institutional Review Board (IRB) — Online Anonymous Survey Consent

Aloha! You are being asked to participate in a research study, “Culturally Protected Area Survey in Vanuatu,” conducted by Dr. Ron Vave, PhD of the Department of Pacific Islands Studies and the Fisheries Graduate Program at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, in collaboration with Rocky Kaku of Vanuatu Fisheries Department.

What am I being asked to do?

If you participate in this project, you will be asked to fill out an online survey about your knowledge of and experience with tabu areas and similar culturally protected area practices in your community.

Taking part in this study is your choice.

Your participation in this project is completely voluntary. You may stop participating at any time. If you stop being in the study, there will be no penalty or loss to you. You may also skip any question you are not comfortable answering.

Why is this study being done?

The purpose of this project is to document tabu areas and similar funerary and culturally protected area practices across Vanuatu, in order to better understand their geographic distribution, cultural significance, and conservation value. We are asking you to participate because you may have direct knowledge of these practices — either as a community member, leader, NGO or government worker, researcher, or someone with first-hand familiarity with tabu areas.

What will happen if I decide to take part in this study?

The survey consists of multiple-choice, short-answer, and open-ended questions about tabu areas and related culturally protected area practices. It takes approximately 30 minutes to complete. The survey is offered in English and may be answered in Bislama where you are most comfortable doing so. We anticipate 100–300 or more people will participate in this study. The survey is accessed on this website at [short URL TBD].

What are the risks and benefits of taking part in this study?

We believe there is little risk to you for participating in this research project. You may become stressed or uncomfortable answering any of the survey questions — for example, when recalling loved ones who have passed on, or when discussing culturally sensitive practices. If you do become stressed or uncomfortable, you can skip the question or take a break. You can also stop taking the survey or withdraw from the project altogether.

There will be no direct benefit to you for participating in this survey. The results of this project may help researchers, communities, NGOs, and government agencies better understand and support customary resource management practices in Vanuatu and the wider Pacific region.

Results of Research:

Community-level findings will be shared in two ways: (1) anonymously through a publicly accessible online dashboard updated as responses are submitted, and (2) in academic journal articles, conference presentations, and reports to NGO and government partners. No individual will be identified in any public output without their separate, explicit written consent. If you provide an optional email address, we may also send you a summary of findings once the project is complete.

Confidentiality and Privacy:

  • One to two sets of names will be requested in this survey: (1) you and/or the informant, and (2) names of loved ones who have passed on for whom a culturally protected area on land or in water was established in the community you are providing information for. As an informant, you will also be asked to share your connection to the community (e.g., community member, researcher, or NGO worker). These names are collected not for publication, but to allow the research team to confirm your connection to, and the place-based legitimacy of, your responses. This step is necessary because the survey is publicly accessible online and could otherwise be completed by individuals with no genuine connection to the communities being documented.
  • Please do not include any other personal information in your survey responses beyond what is specifically asked.
  • Names and identifiers are stored in a secure, password-protected file accessible only to Dr. Ron Vave at the University of Hawaiʻi and Rocky Kaku of Vanuatu Fisheries Department. No individual’s name will appear in any public output, dashboard, or publication without their separate, explicit written consent.
  • Providing your email address is optional. It will only be used if you are open to a follow-up where additional details may be needed, or to send you a summary of findings. It will never be used for any other purpose or shared without your explicit approval.
  • All study data is stored securely by the Vave Lab at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa on password-protected, institutionally backed systems.
  • Other agencies that have legal permission have the right to review research records. The University of Hawaiʻi Human Studies Program has the right to review research records for this study.

Compensation:

There is no monetary compensation or gift for participating in this research project. Your contribution is voluntary, and we are grateful for the time and knowledge you share with us.

Future Research Studies:

Identifiers will be removed from your survey data and, after removal of identifiers, the data may be used for future research studies or distributed to another investigator for future research studies, and we will not seek further approval from you for these future studies.

CPWBs

Culturally protected water body (CPWB) practices are not new to the Pacific. In Vanuatu, a portion of the reef is protected for one month following the circumcision of a boy (Hickey 2006). In Fiji, there are five types; (1) Conception, (2) Meconium, (3) Circumcision, (4) Chiefly installation, and (5) Funerals (Vave 2021).

Other than the funerary protected area information that you’ve already provided information for, this part of the survey is aimed at seeing if the remaining four are also done in the village you’re providing information for in the Solomons.
