Vanuatu Culturally Protected Water Bodies (CPWB) Survey

Help us document kastom tabu areas and culturally protected water bodies in Vanuatu

Across Vanuatu, communities have long protected marine and freshwater areas through kastom — customary practices governed by chiefs (jif) and community elders, often associated with ceremonies held at the nakamal (chiefly meeting house). These ples tabu (tabu areas) are temporarily or permanently closed to harvesting, providing ecological rest for reefs, lagoons, rivers, and coastal waters.

This study documents five types of Culturally Protected Water Bodies (CPWBs) practised in Vanuatu — closures linked to funerals, chiefly installations, circumcision/initiation rites, medicinal harvesting, and conception/birth traditions. Understanding these practices supports their recognition in formal marine protected area (MPA) planning and national conservation policy.

This research is led by Dr. Ron Vave (University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa). It builds on published work on funerary and culturally protected areas in Fiji — see Vave et al. (2024) in Conservation Biology and Vave (2021) in Ambio.

Consent to Participate in Research

University of Hawaiʻi Institutional Review Board (IRB) — Online Anonymous Survey Consent

Halo! You are being asked to participate in a research study, “Culturally Protected Water Bodies (CPWB) and Kastom Tabu Area Survey of Vanuatu,” conducted by Dr. Ron Vave, PhD of the Department of Pacific Islands Studies and the Fisheries Graduate Program at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, in collaboration with Mr. Rocky Kaku of the Vanuatu Fisheries Department.

What am I being asked to do?

If you participate in this project, you will be asked to fill out an online survey about your knowledge of and experience with kastom tabu areas and similar culturally protected water body (CPWB) practices in your community.

Taking part in this study is your choice.

Your participation in this project is completely voluntary. You may stop participating at any time. If you stop being in the study, there will be no penalty or loss to you. You may also skip any question you are not comfortable answering.

Why is this study being done?

The purpose of this project is to document kastom tabu areas and similar culturally protected water body practices across Vanuatu, in order to better understand their geographic distribution, cultural significance, and conservation value. We are asking you to participate because you may have direct knowledge of these practices — either as a community member, jif (chief), NGO or government worker, researcher, or someone with first-hand familiarity with kastom tabu areas.

What will happen if I decide to take part in this study?

The survey consists of multiple-choice, short-answer, and open-ended questions about kastom tabu areas and related culturally protected water body practices. It takes approximately 20–30 minutes to complete. The survey is offered in English, Bislama, and French, and may be answered in whichever language you are most comfortable with. We anticipate 100–300 or more people will participate in this study.

What are the risks and benefits of taking part in this study?

We believe there is little risk to you for participating in this research project. You may become stressed or uncomfortable answering any of the survey questions — for example, when recalling loved ones who have passed on, or when discussing culturally sensitive practices. If you do become stressed or uncomfortable, you can skip the question or take a break. You can also stop taking the survey or withdraw from the project altogether.

There will be no direct benefit to you for participating in this survey. The results of this project may help researchers, communities, NGOs, and government agencies better understand and support customary resource management practices in Vanuatu and the wider Pacific region.

Results of Research:

Community-level findings will be shared in two ways: (1) anonymously through a publicly accessible online dashboard updated as responses are submitted, and (2) in academic journal articles, conference presentations, and reports to NGO and government partners. No individual will be identified in any public output without their separate, explicit written consent. If you provide an optional email address, we may also send you a summary of findings once the project is complete.

Confidentiality and Privacy:

  • One to two sets of names will be requested in this survey: (1) you and/or the informant, and (2) names of loved ones who have passed on for whom a culturally protected area on land or in water was established in the community you are providing information for. As an informant, you will also be asked to share your connection to the community (e.g., community member, researcher, or NGO worker). These names are collected not for publication, but to allow the research team to confirm your connection to, and the place-based legitimacy of, your responses. This step is necessary because the survey is publicly accessible online and could otherwise be completed by individuals with no genuine connection to the communities being documented.
  • Please do not include any other personal information in your survey responses beyond what is specifically asked.
  • Names and identifiers are stored in a secure, password-protected file accessible only to Dr. Ron Vave at the University of Hawaiʻi and Mr. Rocky Kaku of the Vanuatu Fisheries Department. No individual’s name will appear in any public output, dashboard, or publication without their separate, explicit written consent.
  • Providing your email address is optional. It will only be used if you are open to a follow-up where additional details may be needed, or to send you a summary of findings. It will never be used for any other purpose or shared without your explicit approval.
  • All study data is stored securely by the Vave Lab at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa on password-protected, institutionally backed systems.
  • Other agencies that have legal permission have the right to review research records. The University of Hawaiʻi Human Studies Program has the right to review research records for this study.

Compensation:

There is no monetary compensation or gift for participating in this research project. Your contribution is voluntary, and we are grateful for the time and knowledge you share with us.

Future Research Studies:

Identifiers will be removed from your survey data and, after removal of identifiers, the data may be used for future research studies or distributed to another investigator for future research studies, and we will not seek further approval from you for these future studies.

CPWB Practice Details

For each type of culturally protected area you know about, please provide details. These modules expand when you indicate knowledge of that practice.

FPA Funerary Protected Area
A tabu area declared after the death of a chief (jif) or respected community member. The reef, lagoon, river, or coastal waters near the deceased's community or home are closed to fishing and harvesting. The closure lasts until a commemorative feast is held. This practice allows marine resources to recover while honouring the deceased.

Please select an option.

CIPA Chiefly Installation Protected Area
A tabu area declared during or following a chiefly title-taking ceremony or installation. When a new jif (chief) is installed in a ceremony at the nakamal, certain marine or freshwater areas may be placed under closure as part of the ceremonial protocols. This reinforces the chief's authority over customary marine resources (ROC — Reef Owners Committee or similar).

Please select an option.

CircPA Circumcision / Initiation Protected Area
A tabu area declared during boys' circumcision or kastom initiation rites. In many Vanuatu islands, male initiation ceremonies (kastom initiesen) involve periods of seclusion and restrictions on resource use, including closures of fishing grounds. This practice is culturally significant and varies considerably by island.

Please select an option.

MecPA Medicinal / Healing Protected Area
A tabu area closed for traditional healing ceremonies or to protect marine/freshwater species used in kastom medicine. Traditional healers (dokta kastom) may require protected water sources or specific reef organisms. The closure ensures the sanctity and availability of medicinal resources.

Please select an option.

ConcPA Conception / Birth Protected Area
A tabu area closed in connection with conception, pregnancy, or birth traditions (kastom blong bun pikinini). Certain marine or freshwater areas may be placed under closure during pregnancy or after childbirth, protecting both the mother/child and marine resources. The closure may be associated with specific species taboos or ceremonial protocols.

Please select an option.
